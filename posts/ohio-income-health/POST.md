# Where JobsOhio's grant money landed in Ohio

Yesterday we focused on [who got JobsOhio's grants](https://www.data4thepeople.com/p/jobsohio-grants). Today we focus on where.

But before we get to that, we need some additional context to fully appreciate why it's so important where we are making investments. This context comes from a single question: Should economic development be a separate initiative from public health? Ohio’s decision to carve its economic development arm out from state government suggests it views these as separate and distinct tasks. But are they? 

This is a question we will explore in much more depth this Friday. But today, we will release a sneak peek of this work, specifically for Ohio. The GIF below cycles through a handful of scatter plots comparing a health measure to median household income for 3,115 census tracts in Ohio. The health numbers are CDC estimates built partly from each tract's makeup, not counts. These charts suggest a strong link between community health outcomes and community income.

![Animated scatter plot that cycles through seven health measures for 3,115 Ohio census tracts, with median household income on the horizontal axis and the share of adults on the vertical axis. In every frame the dots are highest at low incomes and fall as income rises, flattening above about $100,000. R² by measure: food insecurity 0.69, no leisure-time physical activity 0.69, smoking 0.66, short sleep 0.64, obesity 0.59, diabetes 0.53, loneliness 0.50.](images/01-income-health-ohio.gif)
*Income: Census Bureau, American Community Survey 2020-2024. Health: CDC PLACES 2025, model-based estimates. R² is from a fit on the log of income.*

To be clear, we are not trying to prove this today (even though to us, the connection between income and health seems very logical) but rather pose this question. Should a program like JobsOhio consider that there could be health benefits to its economic development efforts? Could lifting up our most vulnerable communities by making investments in them to bring jobs, and higher incomes, improve health outcomes, and in turn lower the health care burden for the state?

It’s at least a question worth asking. Because if the answer is yes, the map below suggests JobsOhio's grants are not aimed that way now. A different approach could be to target the communities that are in need of most help, and work towards bringing industry where Ohio wants it to go: where it can add the most value. Easier said than done, yes. We present this to show how complex this problem is. Money and health may go hand in hand, up to a point at least. And if they do, this problem of how to drive economic development with precision needs some thought. The question then is whether the current structure of JobsOhio allows for that, or whether changes are needed to make sure that Ohio’s economic development efforts are not blind to their public health impact.
