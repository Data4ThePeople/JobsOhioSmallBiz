# Title

Yesterday we published a tool that lets you explore every JobsOhio grant between 2014 and 2025, and where there public records, see how it performed versus its initial expectations. The tool was designed for resarchers, journalists, and the general public, to explore these grants, understand how many went to small vs. large companies, and understand when the projects didn’t perform. We will leave the exploration of the tool to these folks, and welcome feedback or questions on how to use the tool to better understand these grants.

Today we flip to the next chapter in this story, which we expect to fully unfold over the next two and half weeks.

This chapter starts with a single question: Should economic development be a separate initiative from public health? Ohio’s decision to carve its economic development arm out from state government suggests it views these as separate and distinct tasks. But are they? 

This is a question we will explore in much more depth this Friday. But today, we will release a sneak peek of this work, specifically for Ohio. The gif below cycles through a handful of scatter plots comparing a health measure to median household income for all 3,115 census tracts in Ohio. These charts suggest that there is a meaningful correlation between community health outcomes and community income.

![Animated scatter plot that cycles through seven health measures for 3,115 Ohio census tracts, with median household income on the horizontal axis and the share of adults on the vertical axis. In every frame the dots are highest at low incomes and fall as income rises, flattening above about $100,000. R² by measure: food insecurity 0.69, no leisure-time physical activity 0.69, smoking 0.66, short sleep 0.64, obesity 0.59, diabetes 0.53, loneliness 0.50.](images/01-income-health-ohio.gif)
*Income: Census Bureau, American Community Survey 2020-2024. Health: CDC PLACES 2025, model-based estimates. R² is from a fit on the log of income.*

To be clear, we are not trying to prove this today (even though to us, it seems highly logical) but rather pose this question. Should a program like JobsOhio consider that there could be health benefits to its economic development efforts? Could lifting up our most vulnerable communities by making investments in them to bring jobs, and higher incomes, improve health outcomes, which then lowers the health care burden for the state?

It’s at least a question worth asking. Because if the answer is yes, we can see that JobsOhio is not doing this now. The following map shows the median income by census tract vs. JobsOhio grants by county. The bug circles are where you would expect them to be if economic development was done in isolation – in the big cities and towns where large employers (who as we learned yesterday, are getting the lions share of the grants) are more likely to want to locate. 

![Animated map of Ohio. Census tracts are shaded by median household income in five equal groups, darker for poorer. Orange circles on each county show JobsOhio grant dollars, alternating between total dollars and dollars per person. By total, the largest are Cuyahoga at $227.7 million, Franklin at $150.3 million and Licking at $145.9 million. Per person, the largest are Licking at $802, Belmont at $566 and Logan at $456.](images/02-jobsohio-map.gif)
*$1.26 billion placed in an Ohio county, FY2014 to FY2025. Source: JobsOhio Form 990, Schedule I. Income and population: Census Bureau, American Community Survey 2020-2024.*

A different approach could be to target the communities that are in need of most help, and work towards bringing industry where Ohio wants it to go – where it can add the most value. Easier said than done, yes. But we just present this to show complex this problem is. Money and health may go hand in hand, up to a point at least. They may be intricately linked. And if they are, this problem of how to drive economic development with precision accuracy needs some thought. And then the question is does the current structure of JobsOhio enable that, or do changes need to be made to ensure that Ohio’s economic development efforts are not blind to their public health impact.
